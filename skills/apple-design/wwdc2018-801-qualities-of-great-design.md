# WWDC 2018-801 (The Qualities of Great Design) notes

This session frames quality as intentional craft: outcomes first, style second.

## Core framing

- Quality is not randomness; it is deliberate design in service of people.
- It should reduce friction in thinking, using, and finishing a task.
- Trust is earned across the whole experience, not added by branding.
- Great quality feels enabling, durable, and humane.

## What designers repeatedly called “quality”

- **Care for the work**: small details are consistent, intentional, and refined.
- **Care for users**: good quality holds even in edge routines and uncommon contexts.
- **Deliberate constraints**: systems that are chosen, not accumulated.
- **Clarity over flourish**: beautiful interfaces without clarity are less useful.
- **Durability over trend**: style should not age into confusion.
- **Positive impact**: the interface should make outcomes easier or more meaningful.

## From “what is quality” to design decisions

Use this chain for each proposal:

1. What problem is this reducing?
2. Which user outcome improves?
3. What friction is removed?
4. What assumption changed?
5. What is intentionally *not* solved in this iteration?

If a proposal cannot answer at least one of 1–3, it is probably decorative.

## Four operational definitions

### Simplicity

- Not about fewer features by default.
- About fitting the likely moment and task intensity.
- Infrequent tasks especially need clear default paths.

### Stunning

- Visual and interaction refinement should clarify and support the task.
- A polished look that hinders speed or understanding is not quality.

### Timelessness

- Prefer structures that survive future edits and platform changes.
- If a design depends on a trend that obscures purpose, it is fragile.

### Positive impact

- Measure whether it helps people do something better in real life.
- Impact should be observable: fewer dropped tasks, faster completion, stronger confidence.

## Techniques from designer interviews (high signal for prompts)

1. **See through other perspectives**
   - Use explicit audience variation before finalizing style decisions.
   - Ask: “What would a tired user, beginner, and power user each interpret here?”
2. **Prototype assumptions**
   - Turn assumptions into hypotheses; test quickly in real context.
   - Keep at least one “what if this is wrong” branch.
3. **Prefer discoverable behavior over repeated instruction**
   - Let interaction teach itself; onboarding should be contextual, not a burden.
4. **Institutionalize feedback**
   - Include a feedback section in AI outputs: what to validate, what likely still confuses.
5. **Own your unknowns**
 - Call out missing contexts explicitly: accessibility, novices, experts, edge routines.
6. **Use explicit constraints in collaboration**
   - Record goals, non-goals, and what is intentionally out of scope.

## Practical quality rubric for AI-generated design proposals

- Is this design better at the core outcome or just visually different?
- Is the intended user path unambiguous on first encounter?
- Are secondary details coherent with the primary task?
- Can this be used without external explanation in first use?
- Can the design intent be explained in one sentence?

### Additional review checks

- Does this reduce user effort in at least one common scenario?
- Does it maintain consistency across states (normal, error, loading, completion)?
- Does it add emotional quality without adding cognitive debt?
- Does it preserve dignity and accessibility at the edge?
- Does it leave room for iterative improvement?
